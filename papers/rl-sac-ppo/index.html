Reinforcement Learning for Steering Control in the Formula Student Driverless Simulator

Structural failure modes of Soft Actor-Critic, and the case for PPO. Six training runs, six collapses — traced to three properties of the algorithm, not bad luck or bad tuning.

Daniel Ortiz Valencia · Dept. of Electrical & Computer Engineering
Scroll ↓

The Question

Can a learned policy steer as well as the hand-coded controller we already trust?

We restricted the learning problem to one decision per step — how far to turn — and set out to answer it honestly. Six configurations of Soft Actor-Critic later, every single one had collapsed. We traced the failures to three structural properties of the algorithm itself, adopted Proximal Policy Optimization instead, and ended up with a validated training environment plus a catalogue of failure modes worth publishing precisely because the field's negative results are so rarely written down.

I — The Setup

Before any learning, we built something that already drives

Random initialization in a racing environment crashes the car within a second of every episode — that's a terrible place to start learning from. So before touching an RL algorithm, we built and validated a reactive controller by hand: a LiDAR-only baseline that clusters the point cloud in O(n) by exploiting the angular scan order of the rotating sensor, steering from the mean lateral cone position. It completes laps. It also weaves on straights, because it has no idea which boundary is which color.

Adding a forward-facing camera fixed that. A YOLO detector trained on the FSOCO dataset classifies cone color, a pinhole ground-plane projection converts pixel detections into metric vehicle-frame coordinates, and nearest-neighbor matching fuses that color label with LiDAR depth. Getting the coordinate frames to agree took three separate corrections found through debug logging — a 1.3 m reference-frame mismatch, swapped projection axes, and a wrong default camera pitch. Once fixed, the fused controller stopped weaving. This reactive stack is what we reuse everywhere below: as the RL environment's ground-truth generator, and as the demonstrator for behavior cloning.

The environment itself exposes a 41-dimensional observation — the six nearest blue and six nearest yellow cones as (x, y, color), plus forward speed, lateral speed, yaw rate, the previous steering command, and target speed. Six cones per side gives roughly 15 m of look-ahead at racing speed, enough to see one full corner coming. The action is a single scalar steering command; throttle stays on the proportional controller so the agent only has to learn one thing.

The unglamorous part. Training needs hundreds of thousands of steps — at the simulator's default 30 Hz, a million steps costs about 9 hours. Cranking AirSim's ClockSpeed to 3× and killing the render viewport pushed the step rate past 1,200 iterations/second, 40× baseline. That speedup has a ceiling: push the clock further and the physics timestep grows enough to break collision detection. Two undocumented bugs cost several real days on top of that — the car sometimes spawned with locked wheels (fixed with a mandatory 2 s settle pause after reset), and it silently ignored throttle because it spawned in neutral gear. Both were found the same way: watching the car refuse to move, and reasoning backward.
II — A Warm Start

Teaching the demonstrator before teaching the agent

Since random exploration crashes immediately, we recorded the hand-coded controller driving laps and trained the policy by supervised regression to imitate it — behavior cloning, producing a pretrained checkpoint the RL agent starts from instead of from scratch. The demonstrator itself went through its own iteration: a Gaussian-weighted lookahead caused phase lag at high loop rates, a geometric pure-pursuit variant ran off-track at corner exits, and lowering the speed target only made things worse by weakening the proportional correction.

The version that actually worked was the simplest one we tried: take the midpoint of the three nearest cones per side, apply a proportional correction with a softer divisor. It hits about one cone per lap — an imperfect but adequate teacher, and the clip below is that exact demonstrator driving the track top-down, no vision or reinforcement learning involved yet.

III — Six Ways SAC Collapsed

Every run learned something. Every run then lost it.

With the environment validated and a warm-start policy in hand, we trained with Soft Actor-Critic — an off-policy actor-critic method suited to continuous control, and the natural first choice. Across six configurations, every apparent success carried a hidden pathology.

Test 13454Strong from 5k–11k steps (reward ≈ +0.71), then entropy collapsed. Auto-recovery failed three times; the pruning script then deleted the best checkpoint.

Two reward bugs cost real time before any of this: throttle hardcoded at 40% let the car hit 17 m/s before doing anything useful, and a signed centerline offset consumed without an absolute value rewarded the agent for drifting left — it looked exactly like a tuning problem until it wasn't.

IV — Why SAC Kept Failing

Not tuning errors. Properties of the algorithm.

Across Tests 9 through 13, the same three failure modes kept recurring — and they're structural, not the result of a bad seed or a wrong hyperparameter.

Replay-buffer poisoning. SAC re-learns from a large memory of past experience. A bad episode — driving through 41 cones, say — persists in the buffer for thousands of future updates with no mechanism to flush it, so the agent keeps re-learning behavior it should have abandoned.
Entropy collapse. The automatic entropy tuner can drive the exploration bonus toward zero, making the actor deterministic. Once deterministic, a small systematic bias — steering 0.1 too far, say — gets reinforced by the critic until the policy outputs full steering lock. This is exactly the pattern at step 11k in Test 13.
Catastrophic forgetting after reward changes. The critic's calibration is tied to one specific reward shape. Change a single weight after loading a checkpoint, and the critic starts predicting against a reality that no longer matches its training — the agent optimizes a mispredicted target and collapses. This is what ended Test 9.7.

In combination, almost any SAC experiment was one bad batch, one entropy step, or one reward tweak away from collapse — and none of the three could be fully prevented by tuning around it.

V — The Move to PPO

A slow algorithm that finishes beats a fast one that collapses

After five collapses across distinct configurations, the conclusion wasn't "tune harder" — it was that SAC's failure mode was structural. Proximal Policy Optimization removes each of the three problems above by design, not by tuning:

The tradeoff is sample efficiency: PPO needs roughly 300k–500k environment interactions against SAC's 50k–100k, which is 8–10 wall-clock hours instead of 1–2 at the ~12–15 steps/second this setup sustains. But a SAC run that collapses produces nothing usable, while a PPO run that finishes — even slowly — produces a model that can actually be evaluated and improved on. That asymmetry is the whole argument for switching.

None of the three SAC checkpoints — the BC baseline, Test 9.6, or the Test 13 step-5000 checkpoint — are competition-ready. All three are useful reference points. The more durable result is the infrastructure itself: a correct environment wrapper, a verified reward signal, a working speed-curriculum callback, a stable telemetry pipeline. The expensive debugging — the wheel-lock spawn, the gear flag, the centerline sign error, the lap counter, the corner-distance metric — is done and doesn't need to be repeated. The next experiment is PPO on this same environment and the same BC starting policy; only the algorithm changes.

Open questions worth chasing next: a clean wall-clock-to-baseline benchmark, whether SAC's sample-efficiency advantage trades against cross-track generalization, whether residual RL on top of the reactive controller transfers cleanly, and how much domain randomization closes the cone-detection sim-to-real gap.

Conclusion

A learned steering policy is achievable — the cost was infrastructure, not model novelty

SAC's repeated collapses followed from off-policy replay, automatic entropy tuning, and a critic whose calibration is tied to the reward function. PPO addresses each of these by construction. We're publishing both the successes and the failures here so other teams can reuse the validated infrastructure and skip straight past the failure modes documented above.
