Incremental LiDAR-Camera Sensor Fusion for Reactive Cone Following

A LiDAR-only baseline weaves on straights because it can't tell which boundary is which color. Fusing in a camera fixes that — at 0.06 m average match accuracy, with near-zero systematic bias.

The Problem

A cone-delimited track, and no map of it

Formula Student Driverless tracks are marked only by colored cones — blue on the left, yellow on the right — with no prior knowledge of the layout. This is the story of building that stack in two stages: a LiDAR-only reactive controller that completes laps but oscillates, and a fused color-aware controller that fixes the oscillation by finally knowing which side is which.

I — The Baseline Problem

Fast clustering, and a controller that can't tell left from right

Raw LiDAR returns arrive as a flat array of points in the sensor frame. Sorted by the rotating scanner's angular order, consecutive points that sit within 0.1 m of each other — calibrated to the ~30 cm base of a regulation FSAE cone — get grouped into the same cluster. That ordering is what makes the whole thing run in O(n): no DBSCAN, no k-nearest-neighbor search, just a single pass exploiting the scan geometry.

Steering from this alone is binary: more cones on the left than the right, steer right, and vice versa. On a curve, the inside boundary is genuinely denser, so this works. On a straight, the cone distribution is roughly symmetric — the sign of that comparison flips almost randomly frame to frame, and the car weaves. That weave is the entire reason the rest of this project exists.

II — Adding Eyes

YOLO knows the color. Simulator cones make it lie about it.

A YOLOv5 detector pretrained on the FSOCO dataset classifies each cone by color, but FSOCO is real-world photography — simulator-rendered cones confused it constantly, with blue cones frequently coming back labeled yellow or orange at medium range. Fixing that meant a three-tier verification cascade layered on top of the raw detection: stripe brightness first (bright means blue, dark means candidate-yellow), a BGR channel-dominance check when brightness is ambiguous, and an HSV hue fallback as the last resort.

~98%color accuracy, close range (< 3 m)
~90%medium range (3–5 m), stripe + HSV fallback
~70%beyond 5 m — a sensor-resolution wall, not a software bug

That 70% ceiling is physical: past 5 m a cone occupies roughly 8×10 pixels in a 640×480 frame, background asphalt dominates the bounding box, and the BGR channels converge to within 24 units of gray — there's no color signal left to recover. The practical fix isn't better software; it's that approaching cones grow larger before they enter the steering-critical near field.

III — Fusing LiDAR and Camera

Metric depth from LiDAR, verified color from the camera

A standard pinhole model unprojects each YOLO detection's bottom-center pixel into a normalized camera ray, then intersects that ray with the ground plane to recover a metric position in the vehicle frame. Camera and LiDAR detections are then matched nearest-neighbor: when a camera projection and a LiDAR cluster land within 1.0 m of each other, the pair fuses — LiDAR keeps the depth (it's the accurate measurement), the camera keeps the color label (it's the verified classification).

Getting there took three separate bugs. A 1.3 m X-axis mismatch between LiDAR's sensor-relative frame and the camera's vehicle-relative frame. Swapped projection axes in an early implementation that used horizontal pixel position for forward distance. And wrong default camera parameters — pitch of −12° instead of 0°, an offset of 0.7 m instead of −0.3 m. All three were found the same way: logging every fused pair and watching the residual offset refuse to go to zero until each one was corrected.
IV — Results

The weave is gone

0.06 maverage camera–LiDAR match distance
±0.03 mresidual systematic offset, after calibration
5–6fused cones per frame, typical

With cones fused and color-labeled, the controller switches from a binary left/right decision to a continuous one: compute the geometric midpoint between the blue and yellow boundary averages, steer proportionally toward it. On straights, where the old binary controller alternated between full-lock left and full-lock right every control cycle, the proportional controller now converges smoothly to zero steering. Both controllers run at the same 20 Hz and the same 4 m/s target speed — color-aware fusion is the only variable that changed, and it's the only variable responsible for the improvement.

A layered fallback keeps the car steering even when the camera fails: fused color-aware control when fused cones are available, the binary density heuristic when they aren't, straight-ahead as the last resort. Next: a quantitative validation pass — lap-completion rate, RMS lateral tracking error, YOLOv5 recall on held-out frames — plus replacing the greedy nearest-neighbor matcher with a data-association filter that handles partial occlusion, and moving this stack onto a physical car.
